Desk Food Truck

Build guide: every step, from buying the parts to the truck on your desk

What you are building. A 3D-printed food truck, 229 x 127 x 155 mm (9 x 5 x 6.1 in). Its whole serving side is a 7.3 inch colour e-paper screen that shows which trucks are at Off the Grid, Levi's Plaza today. It wakes each market morning, fetches a fresh picture over Wi-Fi, and sleeps. It should need charging about once a year.

Your build. Route B (a little soldering) · the 7.3 inch screen · 155 mm tall · printed on a Bambu Lab P1S with AMS.
Edition 1, 12 November 2026.

How to use this guide

  • Tick each box as you finish the step. Steps are numbered straight through, 1 to 52.
  • Do the parts in order. Each part ends with a check that must be true before you go on.
  • The drawings are full pages, turned sideways so they print large. Each step names the drawing it needs.
  • Two rules of order: do not model the truck before the parts are in your hands, and do not close the truck before it has run a week on the bench.
Not final in this edition. The software is still being written, so three steps (marked next edition) describe what happens without the exact command or web address. You will get a new edition with those filled in before your parts arrive. Nothing you buy or print depends on them.

Part 1Before you buy

The truck's board can only join a certain kind of Wi-Fi. Find out now, before spending anything.

  1. Ask whoever runs the office Wi-Fi two questions.
    • "Does our Wi-Fi have a 2.4 GHz band that takes a plain network name and password?" The board cannot use 5 GHz, and it cannot click through a sign-in page.
    • "May I put a small home-made gadget on it? It connects for about 40 seconds each morning."
  2. Write the answers here. Network name: ______________________________   2.4 GHz: yes / no   Allowed: yes / no
    If the answer to either is no, stop and tell me. There are ways round it (a guest network, a phone hotspot at your desk, taking it home), and it is better to choose one before the parts arrive.
Check before part 2The office has a 2.4 GHz network with a plain password, and the truck is allowed on it.

Part 4Learn to solder

Half an hour on a practice kit makes the real board easy. Have Drawing 3 (next page) beside you.

  1. Set up. Tile or mat on the desk, a window open, safety glasses on. Iron in its stand, plugged into the laptop charger. Set it to 350 °C (660 °F) for leaded solder, or 370 °C (700 °F) for lead-free. When it is hot, wipe the tip in the brass wool and melt a little solder onto it so it is shiny.
  2. Make one joint, in four beats (about four seconds).
    • Touch the tip to the pad and the pin together.
    • Count "one, two".
    • Feed solder into the joint, not onto the iron, until it flows into a small cone.
    • Solder away first, then the iron.
  3. Build the practice kit. Compare every joint with the "good and bad" row of Drawing 3 and fix the bad ones: a ball means not hot enough (reheat); a bridge between pins means drag the clean tip through it; a gap means add solder; a dull lump means it moved (reheat).
  4. Stop when you have ten joints in a row that you are happy with. Iron back in its stand, unplug it, wash your hands.
Check before part 5Ten clean practice joints: small, shiny cones that hug the pin.

Part 5Prepare the main board

The only soldering on the real parts: two rows of pins on the FireBeetle. Then one knife cut that decides the battery life.

The battery stays unplugged for this whole part. Never solder with the battery connected.
  1. Fit the pins. The board comes with loose strips of header pins. Take two that match the board's two long rows of holes (snap them to length if they are longer). Push the short ends up through the holes from underneath, so the long ends stick out of the top face (the side with the chips). The 8-wire cable will push onto those long ends. Hold pins and board square in the printed jig.
  2. Solder one corner pin, then check. The board should sit flat on the strips. If it is tilted, reheat that joint and press the board down. Then solder the opposite corner.
  3. Solder every remaining pin in both rows. Clean the tip in the brass wool every few joints. Check each joint against Drawing 3 and fix any bridges.
  4. Cut the low-power pad. Hold the board with the chips facing you and the USB-C socket at the top. Find the small square light (the RGB light, labelled 5/D8). Right under it, above the long flat socket, are two small bright square pads joined by a hair-thin copper trace. (It is item 8 in the "Board Overview" picture on DFRobot's page for this board: search "DFR0654 wiki".) With the craft knife, cut through that thin trace between the two pads: two or three firm strokes. Then check with the multimeter on its continuity (beep) setting that the two pads no longer connect. After the cut the little RGB light only works on USB power; that is expected.
    This cut matters more than any other step. With it, the board sleeps at about 12 µA and a charge lasts about a year or more. Without it, the board sleeps at about 520 µA and a charge lasts about four months.
Check before part 6Both rows of pins soldered with no bridges; the low-power pad cut and tested open.

Part 6Bench build

Everything wired up loose on the desk, no truck yet. At the end of this part the bare screen shows today's trucks. Use Drawings 4 and 5.

  1. Lay the parts out on a clean, soft surface: screen face up with its protective film left on, adapter board, main board, battery (unplugged), the 8-wire cable. Never press on the screen's glass or lean anything on it.
  2. Plug the screen's ribbon into the adapter board. Power off and battery unplugged. Lift the little latch on the adapter's socket with a fingernail or tweezers, slide the ribbon in square and fully home with its contacts facing the way the adapter's instructions show, then press the latch closed. The ribbon may only bend in the 5 mm nearest the glass; never crease it.
  3. Plug the cable into the adapter board.
  4. Push the cable's loose ends onto the main board's pins. Go by the names printed on the adapter board, not by wire colours. Tick each as you connect it.
    Adapter pinWhat it carriesGoes to the FireBeetle pin labelled
    VCC3.3 V power to the screen3V3
    GNDgroundGND
    DINpicture data23 (MOSI)
    CLKdata clock18 (SCK)
    CS"listen to me" select13
    DCdata or command22
    RSTreset the screen21
    BUSYscreen says "still redrawing"14
    PWR (only if your adapter has it)switches the screen's power, so it draws nothing while asleep25 (D2)
    These are the pins the program uses. Waveshare's newer adapters have a ninth pin, PWR; older ones have eight and no PWR, which is fine. Drawings 4 and 5 show eight.
  5. Check the battery's polarity before it ever touches the board. Multimeter on DC volts (the 20 V range or auto). Red probe on the metal contact inside the battery plug that the red wire goes to, black probe on the other: it should read about +3.7 to +4.2 V. Now look at the board's battery socket: it has a + and a − printed beside it. Hold the plug the way it will go in. The red wire must line up with the +.
    If red lines up with −, do not plug it in. Tell me: the fix is to swap the two metal contacts in the plug with a pin, which takes a minute, but it must be done first.
  6. Plug in the USB-C data lead from the board to your Mac. Leave the battery unplugged for now.
  7. Load the program. Open Terminal on your Mac and type these lines, one at a time. The first three are needed only once; the third downloads the tools and takes a few minutes. 
    cd ~/Desktop/foodTruck
python3 -m venv firmware/.venv
firmware/.venv/bin/pip install platformio
cd firmware
.venv/bin/pio run -t upload
    It ends with SUCCESS. If the upload never starts ("Connecting......" for a long time), join the board's pin 0/D5 to GND with a spare wire, tap the RST button, run the last line again, then remove the wire.
  8. Give it the Wi-Fi name and password. On its first start the truck makes its own Wi-Fi hotspot called FoodTruck-Setup.
    • On your phone, join the Wi-Fi network FoodTruck-Setup. A set-up page opens by itself (if not, open a browser and go to 192.168.4.1).
    • Pick the office network from step 2, type its password, and save.
    • The truck leaves set-up, joins the office Wi-Fi and fetches the picture. The password is stored only on the board.
    The set-up hotspot stays up for 5 minutes, then the board sleeps. To open it again later, hold the board's small button marked 27/D4 while you tap RST.
  9. Watch it work. The screen flashes through its colours for about 25 seconds, then shows today's trucks. Unplug the USB-C lead, plug in the battery, press the board's reset button: it should do the same again from the battery alone.

Part 7Power check

This is where you find out the battery will last a year, while everything is still on the bench and easy to fix.

  1. Make the test lead. Take the JST extension lead and cut its red wire in the middle. Strip about 5 mm of insulation from each cut end. Leave the black wire whole.
  2. Put the meter in the gap, with a bypass.
    • Plug the extension lead between the battery and the board.
    • Multimeter: black probe in COM, red probe in the µA / mA socket, dial on µA DC.
    • Clip one probe to each stripped end of the red wire, using the crocodile leads.
    • Clip a spare crocodile lead straight across the two stripped ends as well. This is the bypass.
    Why the bypass. On its µA range the meter can only pass a trickle. While the board is awake it needs a thousand times more, so the bypass carries the current until the board is asleep.
  3. Read the sleep current. Press reset. Let the truck wake, redraw and go quiet; wait a full minute more. Now unclip the bypass. The meter shows the sleep current. Write it here: __________ µA.
    • About 15 µA (anything under 50): good. That is a year or more per charge.
    • About 500 µA: the low-power pad is not fully cut. Redo step 19.
    • About 2,000 µA (2 mA): tell me. DFRobot's own page now quotes this figure for the board after the cut, although an independent measurement of the same board found 11.6 µA. If yours reads 2 mA, a charge would last about six weeks, and the fix is a different main board.
    • Anything else in the hundreds or thousands: stop and tell me the number. The usual cause is the screen's adapter board, and there is a known fix.
    Clip the bypass back on before you press reset or unplug anything.
  4. Remove the test lead and plug the battery straight into the board again.
  5. Leave it running on the bench for a week. It should redraw by itself each market morning (Monday to Thursday). Tick the mornings it was right:   ☐   ☐   ☐   ☐   and the next week   ☐   ☐   ☐
Check before part 8Asleep it draws under 50 µA, and it has shown the right trucks on seven market mornings.

Part 9Print and assemble

The truck is plain plastic: no electronics are fixed to it. Use Drawings 11 and 12.

  1. Print the two long side walls, flat and face down, with their inlaid colour.
  2. Print the rest: chassis, the remaining body, roof lid, four wheels, awning and details. The 229 mm body fits your 256 mm bed in one piece.
  3. Dry-fit everything before any glue. Walls into their grooves, lid on, sled in and out.
  4. Glue the magnets. Let two magnets snap together and mark the two outer faces with a pen. Glue one into a post top and its partner into the lid boss above it, marked faces away from each other, so they attract. Do one pair, test that the lid pulls down, then do the other three.
  5. Glue the body together and fix the wheels to the chassis. A few drops of superglue in each groove; hold until set.
  6. Stick a strip of the 2 mm foam tape along the top edge of the sled.
  7. Peel the protective film off the screen. Last thing before it goes in. Hold the sled by its edges.
  8. Lower the sled into the two channels until it stands on the ledge. Look through the rear slot: the USB-C port should be in the middle of it. Put the roof on.
  9. Put it on your desk and press reset once before closing (lift the lid to reach the button). It should redraw. From tomorrow it wakes by itself.
Check before part 10It updates by itself from inside the closed truck, on the desk where it will live.

Part 10Live with it

  1. Give it two weeks. Note anything that annoys you: the wake time, the layout, a truck name that is cut off. The page's design gets real work now that the hardware runs; tell me what you want.
  2. Keep it out of direct sun. The screen fades in it and PLA softens.

Charging (about once a year)

  • When the battery is nearly empty, the screen shows a red CHARGE ME card and the truck stops updating.
  • Plug a USB-C lead into the slot at the back and leave it overnight. Charge it while you are around the first time.
  • It carries on by itself the next market morning. Lift the lid and press reset if you want it sooner.

ReferenceHow it works, in five drawings

You do not need these to build it. They explain what the truck and the cloud are doing, and why the battery lasts.

  • The morning job runs in the cloud at about 6:30 AM. It reads Off the Grid's list for Levi's Plaza, lays it out as a web page, takes a picture of the page in the screen's six colours, and publishes it.
  • The truck wakes at about 7:00 AM, checks whether the picture changed, downloads and redraws only if it did, then sleeps. It is awake for about 40 seconds a day.
  • The screen holds its picture with no power at all.
  • The battery mostly drains by itself. The estimate is 13 to 19 months per charge; part 7 is where you confirm it.
